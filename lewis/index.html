How to draw a Lewis structure

1. Place atoms

  • Pick an element from the row of element buttons (or type its key, below), then click an empty spot on the pad.
  • Click an existing atom to change it to the selected element. This is handy for fixing a wrong atom without redrawing its bonds.

2. Make bonds

  • Bond to a new atom: press on an atom and drag into empty space. A new atom of the selected element appears, already bonded. This is the fastest way to build: place the central atom, then drag outward for each neighbor.
  • Bond two existing atoms: drag from one atom onto the other.
  • Double and triple bonds: click the middle of a bond. Each click goes single → double → triple → back to single.

3. Add lone pairs

  • Choose : Add lone pair, then click an atom once for each pair (up to 4). The pairs spread themselves around the atom automatically.
  • : Remove lone pair takes one pair off each time you click.

4. Mark formal charges

  • Choose + Charge or − Charge and click the atom. Each click changes the charge by one, so to remove a + you click once with −.
  • Mark the charge on the atom that carries it. You do not need brackets around an ion; the charges on the atoms are what count.

5. Fix mistakes

  • ✥ Move lets you drag atoms to tidy up. ⌫ Erase deletes the atom or bond you click (deleting an atom also deletes its bonds).
  • ↶ Undo (or Ctrl+Z) steps back; Clear starts over.
  • ✧ Clean Up redraws your structure neatly, with bond angles set by VSEPR from the bonds and lone pairs you drew. It never changes your atoms, bonds, lone pairs or charges, so it will not fix a mistake for you, but the shape it gives can be a clue: if water comes out straight, check its lone pairs. Undo puts your drawing back.
  • After placing atoms or pairs, the pad switches back to drawing whenever you pick a new element.

6. Check your work

  • Submit tells you what to fix, and circles the atoms in question in red. Change the drawing and submit again as often as you like.
  • Hint shows the skeleton (which atom bonds to which) if you are unsure how the atoms connect.
  • Show answer gives the correct structure, every equivalent resonance form, and a 3D model you can rotate.
  • Your drawing in 3D (beside the pad, or below it on a phone) builds a ball-and-stick model of whatever you have drawn so far, with your lone pairs shown as purple lobes. It uses your bonds and lone pairs exactly as drawn, so if the shape looks wrong, check them.

Boron, beryllium and silicon are on the element buttons only (the B key is bromine). On a phone or tablet, everything works with your finger the same way it does with a mouse.

Formaldehyde, CH₂O. Valence electrons: C 4 + H 2 × 1 + O 6 = 12.

Count as you go: 3 bonds × 2 = 6 electrons, plus 3 lone pairs × 2 = 6, gives 12 in step 3. Turning a lone pair into a bond in step 4 keeps the total at 12; it just shares those electrons with carbon.
  1. Count the valence electrons. Add the group valence electrons of every atom. For an anion add one electron per negative charge; for a cation subtract one per positive charge. (SO₄²⁻: 6 + 4 × 6 + 2 = 32.)
  2. Build the skeleton. The least electronegative atom usually goes in the center (never H). H and F are always on the outside, with one bond each. For organic molecules, follow the name or condensed formula: CH₃OH is C bonded to O, with H on both.
  3. Connect atoms with single bonds. Each bond uses 2 electrons.
  4. Complete the outer atoms. Give each outer atom lone pairs until it has an octet (H needs only 2 electrons, its one bond).
  5. Put any leftover electrons on the central atom as lone pairs.
  6. Fix a central atom short of an octet by turning a lone pair on a neighbor into a double (or triple) bond. Repeat until it has 8.
  7. Assign formal charges. Formal charge = valence electrons − nonbonding electrons − number of bonds. Mark every nonzero charge. The charges must add up to the overall charge of the ion or molecule.
  8. Expanded octets (S, P, Cl, Br, I, Xe…). Atoms from period 3 down can hold more than 8 electrons. In this course, if forming extra double bonds to such a central atom lowers the formal charges, do it: sulfate is drawn with two S=O and two S–O⁻. Second-period atoms (C, N, O, F) never exceed 8.
  9. Allowed exceptions. Boron and beryllium may have fewer than 8 (BF₃, BeCl₂), and a carbocation carbon has only 6. These are correct as drawn with no forced double bonds.
  10. Resonance. If the double bond or charge could equally sit in more than one place (nitrate, acetate, benzene), draw any one of the equivalent forms. You do not need to draw all of them or the ↔ arrows; the answer screen shows them all.
Quick self-check before submitting: correct total electrons? every H has 2 and every C, N, O, F has 8 (unless it is one of the exceptions)? every nonzero formal charge marked? charges as small as possible, with any negative charge on the more electronegative atom?
